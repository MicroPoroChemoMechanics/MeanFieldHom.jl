Tutorials

Mean-field homogenization replaces a heterogeneous microstructure — a matrix carrying inclusions, pores, or cracks — by an equivalent homogeneous medium with the same overall response. MeanFieldHom computes that response from a handful of ingredients: phase properties, geometries, volume fractions, and a scheme encoding an assumption about how the phases interact.

These pages teach that workflow with worked examples, the underlying equations, and plots you can reproduce line by line. They are grouped by theme; within each group, simplest first.

Fundamentals

Start here. The porous material — a solid riddled with pores — recurs throughout and gets two pages, because it is both the simplest non-trivial microstructure and the one where the choice of scheme matters most.

  1. A first homogenization — build your first RVE and compare the dilute and Mori–Tanaka estimates.
  2. Bounds and classical schemes — Voigt/Reuss bounds, self-consistent, and where every scheme sits between them.
  3. Porous materials and the self-consistent trap — why soft pores break the naive self-consistent iteration.
  4. Porous benchmark: all schemes — every scheme side by side on the canonical porosity sweep, spheres and oblate pores alike.
  5. The differential scheme and path dependence — incremental homogenization and why mixing order matters.

Inclusions and geometries

Beyond the single homogeneous ellipsoid: flat cracks, and composite inclusions that carry no Hill tensor at all and plug into the schemes through their volume-averaged concentration tensors instead.

  • Cracks and crack density — from volume fraction to crack density, and the crack-opening-displacement tensor.
  • Layered spheres — Hervé–Zaoui n-layer localization and layer averages.
  • Layered spheroids: geometry and effective conductivity — the confocal n-layer spheroid, its API, the effective conductivity of a composite reinforced by such particles, the equivalent particle, and the numerical accuracy of the harmonic series.
  • Imperfect interfaces: what they do to the local fields — pointwise temperature and flux, streamlines, an animated sweep over the interface conductance, and an interactive 3-D view.
  • Highly conducting interfaces — the equivalent conductivity of an HC-coated particle against aspect ratio.

Beyond elasticity

  • Viscoelastic composites — complex moduli in the frequency domain, and a first taste of ageing creep.

Differentiation and solvers

  • Derivatives and sensitivities — differentiate any homogenization result with ForwardDiff, no finite differences.
  • From derivatives to a strength criterion — turn those derivatives into a macroscopic strength criterion for a porous solid.
  • Nonlinear solvers for the self-consistent fixed point — solve the fixed point with NonlinearSolve.jl instead of Picard iteration, and check that sensitivities agree regardless of which solver found it.
  • Symbolic spheres — derive the Eshelby/Hill tensors and the dilute, Mori–Tanaka and self-consistent estimates in closed form, then substitute numbers at the very end.

Interoperability and tools

  • From Echoes to MeanFieldHom — translate your Echoes (C++/Python) scripts, and cross-check with Echoes live via PyCall.
  • Symmetrization — exact rotation-group average versus best-fit projection, and when the two differ.

Prerequisites

These tutorials assume you can install and load the package (see Installation) and focus on why each scheme and computation is used, not on restating the API. For a terse reference — the full scheme list, aliases, and keyword arguments — see Homogenization schemes and the other Manual pages. For the underlying derivations, see Theory. Once comfortable here, the Applications section walks through full case studies drawn from published work — cement paste, transport properties, strength, bituminous binders, ageing creep.
